Every website stands on two rented foundations: a **domain name** (your address, like `yourbusiness.co.ke`) and **hosting** (the computer that serves your site to visitors). Most business owners encounter both for the first time when a developer asks them to pay for them — which is a bad time to learn what they are. This guide covers what you're buying, what it should cost, and the ownership mistakes that cause the most pain later.

## Domain names: .co.ke vs .com vs .ke

Your domain is your permanent address on the internet. For a Kenyan business, the realistic options:

- **.co.ke** — the standard commercial Kenyan domain. Signals a local business clearly, and good names are far more available than on .com. For most Kenyan small businesses, this is the default right answer.
- **.ke** — the shorter second-level Kenyan domain (e.g. `yourbusiness.ke`). Newer and shorter; availability is good. Works well if you want something brief for print and signage.
- **.com** — global and universally recognized, but most good names were taken decades ago, and it says nothing about being Kenyan. Worth holding *in addition* if you can get it, rarely the best primary choice for a local business.
- **Others (.or.ke, .ac.ke, .go.ke, .me.ke, .info.ke, .mobi.ke, .ne.ke, .sc.ke)** — restricted or niche extensions for specific uses (organizations, academic institutions, government, individuals). The full list is published by [KeNIC](https://kenic.or.ke/), the registry that administers .KE domains.

Practical advice: secure your business name on .co.ke first. If budget allows, also take the .ke (and the .com if available) and point them at the same site — it prevents impersonators and captures visitors who guess the wrong ending. KeNIC's published figures put .KE registrations in the tens of thousands and growing, so availability is still reasonable — but exact-match names for common business terms go steadily, so don't wait years.

## How registration actually works

You don't buy a domain from KeNIC directly — you register through one of KeNIC's **accredited registrars** (listed on [kenic.or.ke](https://kenic.or.ke/)), who handle the sale and yearly renewal. Key facts owners often miss:

- **Registration is a yearly rental, not a purchase.** You pay every year to keep the name. Let it lapse and it eventually becomes available to anyone — including competitors and squatters.
- **Renewal reminders go to the registered email.** Use a business-controlled email address you will still own in five years, not a developer's address and not a free account you might abandon.
- **Prices are set by registrars**, not the registry, so they vary — but for a standard .co.ke or .com, expect on the order of one to a few thousand shillings per year. Anything dramatically higher deserves a question.
- **Keep your own registrar login.** If your developer registered the domain in their account "to keep things simple," your business address is hostage to that relationship. The domain must be in an account you control, full stop.

## Hosting: what it is and what to choose

Hosting is rented space on a computer (a server) that's always online, serving your site to visitors. When someone types your domain, the domain points them to your hosting, and the hosting delivers the pages.

For a typical small-business site, the options in ascending order of power and complexity:

1. **Shared hosting.** Your site shares a server with others. Cheapest, simplest, and entirely adequate for brochure sites and small stores. Downsides: performance depends on neighbors, and very cheap plans can be slow — speed matters (see [why mobile performance matters](/mobile-friendly-websites-kenya)).
2. **Managed hosting / platform hosting.** The provider handles updates, security, and backups for you. Costs more, removes headaches. A good fit for owners who want zero technical involvement.
3. **VPS or dedicated servers.** Your own slice (or all) of a server. More power and control, but you're responsible for maintenance unless someone manages it for you. Overkill for most small sites; relevant for busy stores and custom systems.

Honest guidance: start with the simplest option that meets your needs, with a host your developer knows and can support. A small brochure site doesn't need a VPS, and paying for one anyway buys nothing but a bigger bill. What matters far more than server size is that *someone competent is responsible for it* — see our [maintenance and security guide](/website-maintenance-security-basics-kenya).

## The ownership checklist (do this whatever you choose)

More businesses are hurt by ownership confusion than by any technical failure:

1. **Domain registered in your account**, with your business email as the contact. Verify by logging in yourself.
2. **Hosting account in your name** (or at minimum, documented access you hold). If the developer insists on their own account, get the arrangement — and the handover process — in writing before paying.
3. **Admin access to the website itself** held by you, not only the developer.
4. **Renewal dates on your calendar.** Domains and hosting renew yearly; set reminders a month ahead. Auto-renewal helps if the payment method stays valid — check it yearly.
5. **Backups you can reach.** Ask where backups live and confirm you (not just the developer) can access them.

## What it should cost

Domains and hosting together typically run to a few thousand shillings per year for a small site — small compared to the build cost (see [website costs in Kenya](/how-much-does-website-cost-kenya)), but recurring forever, so ownership hygiene matters more than the amount. Be suspicious of "free domain and hosting for life" bundled into a build price: it usually means the developer owns both, and leaving them later means rebuilding from scratch.

None of this is complicated once it's written down. The businesses that get hurt are the ones where it never was — a domain in a ex-developer's account, hosting nobody can log into, a site held together by one person's memory. Spend an hour on the checklist above and you'll never be that business.
