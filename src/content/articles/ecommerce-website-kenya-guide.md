"Should we sell online?" usually means "should we build an e-commerce website?" — and the honest answer is: it depends on what you'd be asking that website to do. Selling ten phone accessories a week to people who found you on Instagram is a different problem from running a catalog of 500 products with countrywide delivery. This guide breaks online selling into its actual parts — payments, delivery, trust, and cost — so you can start at the right size and grow deliberately.

## The four parts of selling online

Every online sale, however simple, needs all four. The question is only how formally each is handled:

1. **Discovery** — how customers find the product (your site, Google, social media, marketplaces).
2. **Payment** — how money moves from customer to you securely.
3. **Fulfillment** — how the product reaches the customer (delivery, pickup, digital delivery).
4. **Trust** — why a stranger believes they'll actually receive what they paid for.

A full e-commerce website handles all four in one place. But many Kenyan businesses profitably handle discovery on Instagram, payment via M-Pesa Till, fulfillment via a courier or matatu parcel service, and trust via reviews and consistent delivery — with the "website" being a simple catalog page plus WhatsApp ordering. That's not a lesser version of e-commerce; for low volumes, it's often the *better* one. Build the formal system when volume justifies it, not before.

## Payments: M-Pesa first, cards second

For Kenyan customers, M-Pesa isn't a payment option — it's *the* payment option. Any online store that makes M-Pesa awkward will lose sales it never hears about. The levels, in order of sophistication:

- **Manual Till/PayBill + confirmation.** Customer pays, sends confirmation, you verify and dispatch. Fine for a handful of orders a week; breaks down with volume (see below).
- **Integrated checkout (STK Push).** Customer enters their number, approves the prompt on their phone, and the order confirms automatically. The professional standard once orders are daily. Our [M-Pesa integration guide](/mpesa-integration-for-business-websites) explains how this works technically.
- **Cards for international and corporate buyers.** Worth adding if you sell to customers outside Kenya or to businesses — but don't lead with cards for a Kenyan audience.

The tipping point from manual to integrated is usually one of: reconciliation eating real time daily, missed or disputed payments, or customers abandoning because paying feels uncertain. If any of those sound familiar, integration will pay for itself quickly.

## Delivery: decide the policy before the technology

Delivery kills more Kenyan online stores than technology does. Before building anything, answer in writing:

- **Where do you deliver?** Your town only? Countrywide? Exact areas, not "everywhere."
- **How long does it take?** Realistic ranges per zone — and what happens when you're late.
- **What does it cost, and who pays?** Flat rate, by zone, free above a threshold? Vague delivery costs abandon more carts than prices do.
- **Who carries it?** Courier partner, rider, parcel service, customer pickup — named, with a backup for busy periods.
- **What if it fails?** Wrong address, customer unreachable, damaged goods — decide the policy before the first incident forces one.

Put the answers on the site as a delivery page customers can read *before* ordering. Stores that hide delivery terms until checkout pay for it in abandoned carts and angry messages.

## Trust: the actual conversion work

Strangers don't send money to websites they don't trust. The elements that persuade, roughly in order of impact:

- **Real product photos** — your actual stock, multiple angles, honest condition. Stock photos on a store selling physical goods read as a scam signal.
- **Clear, all-in pricing** — price plus delivery cost visible early. "Call for price" on a store is a conversion killer.
- **Reviews from past buyers** — Google reviews for the business, product-level feedback where possible. See [how to build reviews](/get-online-reviews-kenya-business).
- **A physical location or verifiable identity** — address, phone number, business name, WhatsApp line. Anonymous stores don't get the benefit of the doubt.
- **A stated returns policy** — even a narrow one ("wrong or damaged items replaced within 7 days") converts better than silence.
- **HTTPS and professional presentation** — baseline expectations; see [what a good business website includes](/what-good-business-website-includes).

## What it costs

Honest ranges are hard because scope varies so much, but the shape is consistent: a simple catalog-plus-WhatsApp site costs roughly what any small business site costs; a full store with integrated M-Pesa checkout, order management, and delivery logic costs significantly more to build and to maintain. See our [website cost breakdown](/how-much-does-website-cost-kenya) for indicative SmartBiz rates by project type, and budget for the ongoing side too — [maintenance](/website-maintenance-security-basics-kenya), transaction costs, delivery operations, and the customer-service time every order generates. The build price is the entry ticket, not the total cost.

## Start lean, grow on evidence

A sensible path for most businesses:

1. **Validate demand first.** Sell manually (social + M-Pesa + WhatsApp) until orders are regular. If you can't sell ten items manually, a website won't sell a hundred.
2. **Add a catalog site with WhatsApp ordering.** Professional presentation and discovery, human checkout. This carries many businesses further than expected.
3. **Integrate payments** when manual reconciliation hurts — STK Push checkout on your own store.
4. **Expand fulfillment** (more zones, courier integrations, order tracking) as volume demands.

Each step should be funded by revenue from the previous one. E-commerce rewards patience and punishes premature scale — the failed stores we see usually built step 4 infrastructure for step 1 demand. Start where your orders are, not where your ambitions are, and let the evidence pull you forward.
