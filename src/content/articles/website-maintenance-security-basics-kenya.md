Launching a website feels like finishing. It isn't — it's the start of ownership. An unmaintained site decays: software goes unpatched, content goes stale, backups don't exist until the day they're needed, and small problems compound quietly until something visible breaks. This guide covers what "maintenance" actually means, what's genuinely important versus upsell, and how to make sure someone responsible is doing it.

## Why maintenance matters (the non-technical version)

- **Security.** Most website hacks don't target *you* — automated bots probe millions of sites for known vulnerabilities in outdated software. Patching promptly is the difference between being a hard target and an easy one.
- **Trust.** A hacked site that redirects visitors to spam, shows a "not secure" warning, or displays prices from 2023 actively repels customers. Your site is often checked *after* a referral — decay undoes word of mouth.
- **Search visibility.** Google notices hacked, broken, or painfully slow sites, and demotes them. Recovery from a hack or a malware flag takes far longer than prevention would have.
- **Continuity.** Hosting renewals lapse, domains expire, payment methods fail. Maintenance is partly just *someone paying attention* before small lapses become outages.

## The essential checklist

Whatever platform your site runs on, these six items cover nearly all of it:

1. **Updates applied promptly.** Your site's software, themes/plugins, and server software all receive security updates. "Promptly" means days to weeks for security releases, not months. Ask your developer what the update process is and who triggers it.
2. **Backups that exist and work.** Automated backups, stored somewhere separate from the site itself, tested by actually restoring one occasionally. A backup nobody has ever restored is a hope, not a plan. Confirm you can access backups independently of your developer.
3. **HTTPS always on.** The padlock. Standard on modern hosting, must renew automatically (certificates expire — usually every few months — and auto-renewal should handle it). If your site ever shows "not secure," treat it as urgent.
4. **Strong, unique credentials.** Admin passwords long and unique, logins limited to people who need them, ex-staff and ex-developers removed. Enable two-factor authentication wherever the platform supports it. Most small-site breaches start with a reused or shared password, not sophisticated hacking.
5. **Uptime and issue monitoring.** Someone should know the site is down before your customers tell you. At minimum: [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console/about) set up (Google emails you about indexing and security issues it detects) and an agreement with your developer about response times.
6. **Content freshness.** Hours, prices, phone numbers, team, portfolio — reviewed quarterly. Stale content is a slow trust leak; schedule the review rather than relying on memory.

## What a fair maintenance plan covers

Many developers (including [SmartBiz](https://smartbiz365.site/) — see indicative rates in our [website cost breakdown](/how-much-does-website-cost-kenya)) offer monthly maintenance plans. A fair one should name, in writing:

- What's included: updates, backups, security checks, uptime monitoring, and typically a small allowance of content changes per month.
- Response times: how quickly problems get acknowledged and fixed.
- What's *excluded*: new features, redesigns, and major content overhauls are project work, not maintenance — the plan should say so plainly.
- How to leave: you keep your domain, hosting access, site files, and backups if you cancel. Anything else is a hostage arrangement, not a service.

Red flags: plans that can't say what's actually done each month, "maintenance" priced per tiny change with no allowance, and any setup where cancelling means losing your site. Our [domain and hosting guide](/domain-hosting-guide-kenya) covers the ownership side in detail.

## DIY vs. paying someone

If your site is a simple brochure site on managed hosting, the DIY burden is small: keep logins safe, review content quarterly, keep Search Console and renewals monitored, and pay a developer for an annual checkup. If your site takes payments, holds customer data, or runs on software needing frequent updates, professional maintenance pays for itself the first time it prevents (or quickly fixes) one incident. Match the arrangement to the stakes: the costlier downtime or a breach would be, the less sense DIY makes.

## A minimal routine that covers most businesses

| Frequency | Action |
|---|---|
| Monthly | Confirm backups ran; glance at Search Console for new issues |
| Quarterly | Review all content for staleness; test contact forms and checkout |
| Yearly | Verify domain/hosting renewals and payment methods; review who has access; run [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) and fix regressions |

Put the quarterly review on a calendar now. An hour four times a year prevents nearly every "how long has the site been like this?" conversation — and those conversations are always more expensive than the hour would have been.
