Eldoret has grown into one of Kenya's busiest regional trade and agricultural hubs, and the businesses competing for customers here — from hotels and hardware stores to law firms and salons — increasingly win or lose that competition online before a customer ever walks through the door. A website built without Eldoret's specific market in mind, though, usually underperforms one that is.

## Eldoret's Market Isn't Nairobi's Market

Web design advice online is overwhelmingly written for Nairobi or for generic "African markets" content, and a lot of it doesn't quite fit here. Eldoret customers searching for a service are more likely to be searching by neighborhood or landmark ("hardware shop near Huruma," "hotel near Eldoret town") than by brand name, and word-of-mouth referrals still drive a large share of local business — which means your website's job is often less about winning cold traffic and more about *confirming* a referral once someone looks you up. That changes what matters: a clear address and working phone number, real photos of your actual premises, and a page that loads fast on a mid-range Android phone matter more here than flashy animation.

## Most of Your Visitors Are on a Phone, and Data Isn't Free

According to the Communications Authority of Kenya's most recent sector statistics report, the country now has over 88 million active mobile subscriptions and more than 52 million connected smartphones, with smartphones making up roughly two-thirds of all connected devices and mobile broadband accounting for the large majority of internet access. In practice, that means a website built and tested on a desktop browser, then assumed to "work fine" on mobile, is being judged by the wrong standard. It also means every megabyte matters — mobile data in Kenya isn't free, so a heavy, unoptimized homepage doesn't just load slowly, it costs your visitor money to even see it. Fast, lightweight, mobile-first design isn't a nice-to-have for an Eldoret business; it's the baseline.

## Getting Found by Customers Searching Nearby

A website that exists but doesn't show up when someone in Eldoret searches for what you sell isn't doing its job. The highest-leverage step most local businesses skip is a properly filled-out Google Business Profile — accurate hours, your real location pinned on the map, your phone number, and photos — paired with a website that actually mentions Eldoret and your specific neighborhood in its text (not just your business name). Genuine customer reviews matter more here than most owners realize: they're often the deciding factor between two similar businesses in a Google Maps search.

## What to Look for in a Web Developer

Not every developer who can build *a* website can build one that works for a local Eldoret business. Worth checking before you hire:

- **Relevant portfolio** — have they built for businesses similar to yours, ideally in Kenya, and can you see the live sites (not just screenshots)?
- **SEO literacy** — can they explain, in plain terms, how they'll help you show up in local search, or is SEO an afterthought?
- **Realistic timelines and pricing** — vague quotes and "it depends" with no follow-up detail are a warning sign.
- **A support plan after launch** — a website needs occasional updates, security patches, and content changes; ask who handles that and at what cost once the initial project ends.
- **Straightforward communication** — how they respond during the sales conversation is usually how they'll communicate during the project.

## Questions Worth Asking Before You Sign

1. Will the site be built mobile-first, and can I see it on a phone before final payment?
2. Who owns the domain name and hosting account once the project is done?
3. What happens if I want to add or change content myself later — can I, or do I need to pay for every edit?
4. Is there a maintenance or support plan, and what does it cost?
5. Will you help set up or optimize my Google Business Profile as part of the project?

Getting clear answers to these up front avoids the most common regret local business owners report after a rushed website project: paying for something that looks fine but doesn't actually bring in customers.
