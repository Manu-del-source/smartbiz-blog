Plenty of business websites exist without actually working. They're online, they look reasonable at a glance, and they still don't bring in customers. Usually the gap isn't design polish — it's a handful of specific, practical elements that got skipped. Use this as a checklist, whether you're briefing a developer or auditing your own site.

## 1. A clear answer to "what do you do?" — above the fold

A visitor should understand what you offer and who it's for within seconds of landing on your homepage, without scrolling or decoding a paragraph of mission-statement language. "We supply and install solar systems for homes and businesses in Uasin Gishu" beats "Welcome to our website — your trusted partner in excellence" every time, for both humans and search engines.

**How to verify:** show the homepage to someone unfamiliar with your business for five seconds, then ask what you sell. If they can't say, the headline has failed.

## 2. Real contact information, including WhatsApp

A phone number, physical location if relevant, and a clickable WhatsApp link — visible from the homepage, not buried three clicks deep in a "Contact Us" page. For many Kenyan customers, tapping straight through to WhatsApp is the preferred way to reach a business, and hiding that option behind a contact form loses a real share of inquiries.

At minimum: a tap-to-call number in the header on mobile, a WhatsApp button that's visible without scrolling, your location or service area in plain text, and your hours. Forms are fine as a secondary option; they should never be the *only* option.

## 3. Genuine mobile responsiveness

Not "technically viewable on a phone," but designed for one: buttons large enough to tap, text readable without zooming, no horizontal scrolling, and the essential information (what you sell, how to reach you) near the top rather than below a giant banner image. With the large majority of Kenyan internet access happening over mobile, this isn't optional polish — see [why mobile-friendly design matters](/mobile-friendly-websites-kenya) for the full reasoning.

**How to verify:** open the site on an actual mid-range Android phone on mobile data — not just your developer's laptop, not just a desktop browser's "mobile view." If it frustrates you, it frustrates your customers.

## 4. Fast loading on mobile data

Every unnecessary megabyte costs your visitor time and, in Kenya specifically, real money in data costs. Compressed, properly-sized images and a lean page directly affect how many visitors stick around long enough to see what you offer — and Google treats speed as a ranking consideration too.

**How to verify:** run the homepage through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/), Google's free speed test. Pay attention to the mobile score, not just desktop. If images are flagged, that's usually the single biggest and cheapest fix.

## 5. Trust signals

New visitors decide within seconds whether you look legitimate. The signals that actually persuade:

- **Genuine customer reviews or testimonials** — specific ones with names and details, not anonymous generic praise. Link to your Google reviews where possible.
- **Real photos** of your actual work, team, or premises — stock imagery actively undermines trust once visitors notice it.
- **A stated physical location or service area**, if applicable — "we serve Eldoret and surrounding areas" beats a site with no location at all.
- **Years in operation or registration details**, where relevant to the industry.
- **Examples of past work** with enough detail to be verifiable.

One honest signal beats five vague ones. "Serving Eldoret since 2019 — 200+ solar installations across Uasin Gishu" is verifiable and specific; "we are the leading provider of quality solutions" is neither.

## 6. A clear call to action on every page

Every page should make it obvious what you want the visitor to do next: call, message on WhatsApp, request a quote, or make a purchase. A website with no clear next step leaves an interested visitor to figure it out themselves, and many won't bother.

Match the action to the page. A services page should offer "request a quote." A product page should offer "order on WhatsApp" or "add to cart." The homepage should offer the single most common path your customers take. One primary action per page, repeated where the page is long — not five competing buttons.

## 7. Basic SEO set up from the start

Proper page titles, headings that describe what's actually on the page, and content that mentions your location and services in normal language. This isn't advanced work — it's the foundation everything else builds on, and retrofitting it later costs more than doing it during the build. The fundamentals are covered in [how to get found on Google](/how-to-get-found-on-google-kenya), and Google's own [SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) is the authoritative reference worth bookmarking.

When briefing a developer, ask specifically: "what SEO setup is included in this price?" If the answer is vague, it isn't included.

## 8. HTTPS and basic security

A visible padlock (HTTPS) is now a baseline expectation — browsers actively flag sites without it as "not secure," which damages trust before a visitor reads a word. This is standard on virtually all modern hosting and shouldn't be treated as optional or as an upsell. Beyond that, ask your developer about the basics covered in our [maintenance and security guide](/website-maintenance-security-basics-kenya): updates, backups, and who handles them after launch.

## Scoring your own site

Go through the eight items above and mark each as done, partial, or missing. Most underperforming business websites aren't missing one dramatic thing — they're missing three or four items, each individually small, adding up to a site that looks fine but doesn't convert visitors into customers. Fix the missing ones in the order listed: clarity and contacts first, polish last.
