The Communications Authority of Kenya's quarterly sector statistics reports (published on [ca.go.ke](https://www.ca.go.ke/)) have consistently shown active mobile subscriptions in the tens of millions, with smartphones making up roughly two-thirds of connected mobile devices and mobile broadband carrying the large majority of internet subscriptions. Figures update quarterly, so check the latest report — but the direction hasn't changed in years. For a Kenyan business, that isn't a background statistic. It's a description of who's going to visit your website, and on what: a phone, on mobile data, quite possibly a mid-range Android device a few years old.

## "Mobile-friendly" means more than "it doesn't look broken"

A lot of websites technically render on a phone without being genuinely built for one. The difference between a desktop layout shrunk to fit and a site designed mobile-first:

- **Thumb-sized targets.** Buttons and links sized for a finger, spaced so tapping "Call now" doesn't also hit the menu. Anything requiring pinch-to-zoom has failed.
- **Prioritized content.** The one thing a mobile visitor came for — your phone number, location, menu, price list — near the top, not three scrolls below a full-screen banner image.
- **Readable without effort.** Text sized for a small screen at arm's length, with enough contrast to read outdoors in bright light.
- **No hover-dependent anything.** Menus, image galleries, or details that only appear on mouse hover simply don't exist for touch users.

If your developer shows you the site on a laptop and says "it's responsive," that answers the easy half. The hard half is whether it's *usable* on the device your customers actually own.

## Data costs money — page weight is a cost to your visitor

Mobile data in Kenya isn't free, and while 4G coverage and speeds have improved significantly, a heavy homepage still costs your visitor real shillings to load — every single time. This is the most overlooked part of mobile design: it's not just layout, it's how much data the page consumes. Oversized images, autoplaying video, and unnecessary tracking scripts all add weight a desktop-focused developer never notices on office Wi-Fi, but a visitor on a limited bundle absolutely feels.

As a rule of thumb: every image on the site should be compressed and sized to its actual display dimensions *before* upload. A 4MB camera photo displayed at 400 pixels wide is pure waste — often 90%+ reducible with no visible difference. This single discipline is usually the biggest factor in page weight.

## Practical things that actually move the needle

- **Compress and properly size every image** before it goes on the site. If your site has a content system, make this part of the upload habit, not a one-time fix.
- **Design tap targets for fingers, not cursors.** Key actions — call, WhatsApp, directions, order — as large, thumb-reachable buttons.
- **Put essentials first.** Contact details, what you sell, and how to reach you should survive a three-second glance at the top of the page.
- **Add click-to-call and click-to-WhatsApp buttons.** On mobile, a tappable number converts far better than a "Contact Us" page buried in the menu.
- **Cut what the phone doesn't need.** Autoplay video, animated sliders, and widgets that seemed nice on desktop are usually the first things to go — they cost data, battery, and patience.
- **Keep forms short.** Every field you demand on a phone keyboard loses people. Ask for the minimum (often just name, phone number, and message) and collect the rest in conversation.

## Test the way your customers browse

Don't accept "it works on mobile" without evidence. A practical test protocol:

1. Open the site on an actual mid-range Android phone, on mobile data — not Wi-Fi, not a flagship, not a desktop browser's "mobile view."
2. Time how long the homepage takes to become usable. If you're counting past a few seconds, so are your visitors.
3. Try the main customer journey (find the number, the menu, the booking button) one-handed.
4. Run the homepage through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) and read the mobile score and its specific recommendations.

If your developer resists any of this, that tells you something about the developer. Mobile-first isn't a feature to add later — it's the baseline the whole build should be judged against.
