"How much does a website cost?" doesn't have one honest answer, because a landing page with five sections of text and an e-commerce store with M-Pesa checkout, inventory management, and delivery tracking are not the same project — even though they're both technically "a website." What follows are realistic ranges, what moves the price between them, and how to compare quotes without getting burned.

## What actually drives the price

- **Scope and design complexity.** A single landing page with your services, prices, and contact details is one design problem. A twelve-page site with distinct layouts, photo galleries, and a blog is twelve of them. Every custom layout is design and build time.
- **Functionality.** Anything the site *does* beyond displaying information adds development time: a booking system, e-commerce checkout, M-Pesa integration via the Daraja API, a customer login area, automated emails. Each is a feature to build, test, and maintain — see [what M-Pesa integration actually involves](/mpesa-integration-for-business-websites) for one example.
- **Content.** Whether you're supplying finished copy and good photos, or need those written, photographed, and organized as part of the project. Many projects stall for weeks waiting on client content; some developers include copywriting, others charge extra for it. Ask.
- **Ongoing costs.** A domain name and hosting renew every year (typically a few thousand shillings a year combined for a small site — check current registrar pricing, as it changes), plus whatever maintenance arrangement you choose. These are separate from the build price in almost every quote. Our [domain and hosting guide](/domain-hosting-guide-kenya) explains what you're actually paying for.

## Realistic price ranges

The table below shows [SmartBiz's](https://smartbiz365.site/) own current published rates, shared as one realistic reference point rather than a universal price list — other developers price differently based on their process and experience, and our own rates change over time, so treat these as indicative and confirm with an actual quote:

| Project type | Typical price (SmartBiz indicative rates) |
|---|---|
| Landing page (single page) | KES 15,000 |
| Business website (multi-page) | KES 30,000 |
| Advanced website (custom features) | KES 50,000 |
| E-commerce store | KES 60,000 |
| Custom SaaS / business system | From KES 100,000 |
| Ongoing maintenance | KES 5,000/month |
| Hourly work | KES 2,000/hour |

As a rough market-wide observation: simple brochure sites from freelancers and small studios commonly fall in the tens of thousands of shillings, while e-commerce and custom systems run higher depending on features. Quotes far outside these bands in either direction deserve scrutiny, not automatic rejection — just make sure you understand *why*.

## Why the cheapest quote isn't always the best value

A very low quote usually means one of a few things: a barely-customized template, no real SEO setup, no mobile optimization beyond the default, stock photos instead of your own, or no support once the site is delivered. None of that is automatically a bad choice on a tight budget — but it's worth knowing what you're trading away rather than discovering it later, when the site doesn't rank on Google, looks broken on a phone, or can't be updated without paying again.

The mirror image matters too: a high quote isn't automatically better. You're paying for outcomes — [the elements that make a site actually work](/what-good-business-website-includes), delivered reliably — not for the developer's rent. Judge the quote by what it includes, in writing.

## Fixed price vs. growing price

The most common pricing complaint we hear isn't about the number — it's that the number grew mid-project as "extras" appeared. Protect yourself with one question asked upfront: *is this quote fixed for the agreed scope, and what happens when I request something outside it?* A professional answer names the scope in writing and prices changes transparently. Vague answers here are the clearest warning sign in the entire process.

## A checklist for comparing two quotes

Get both developers to answer the same questions, in writing. If your business is in Eldoret, our [Eldoret website guide](/website-design-eldoret-guide) adds local-market specifics — what to look for in a developer and how nearby customers search — to the questions below:

1. Does the price include mobile optimization and basic SEO setup, or are those extra?
2. Who owns the domain and hosting account after the project ends — me, or you?
3. What happens if I need changes after launch — is a support period included, and what does ongoing [maintenance](/website-maintenance-security-basics-kenya) cost?
4. Is the quoted price fixed for the stated scope, and how are out-of-scope requests priced?
5. Will I be able to update basic content myself, or does every edit require paying you?
6. What is the timeline, and what do you need from me (and when) to hit it?

Two quotes answered against the same six questions become genuinely comparable. Without that, you're just picking the smaller number and hoping — which is how businesses end up paying twice: once for the cheap site, once for its replacement.
